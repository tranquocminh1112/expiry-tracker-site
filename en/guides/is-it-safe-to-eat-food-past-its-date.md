# Is it safe to eat food past its expiration date?

Crackers two weeks past their date, yogurt one day past: the answer depends on two things: **what kind of date is on the label**, and **what kind of food it is**.

This guide summarizes advice from food safety agencies. It's general information, not medical advice. If you're cooking for babies, pregnant women, older adults or anyone with a weakened immune system, be more cautious than the rules below.

> **The short rules**
>
> - **Past "use by" on fresh, perishable food** (UK, EU, Australia): throw it out, even if it looks and smells fine.
> - **Past "best before" on long-life food:** usually fine if the pack is intact, it was stored properly and it shows no signs of spoilage.
> - **In the US**, most dates are about quality. For perishables, go by safe storage times rather than the printed date.
> - **Bulging or leaking cans, mold on soft food, perishables left out more than 2 hours:** throw them out, whatever the date says.
> - **When in doubt, throw it out.**

## Why "looks fine" isn't enough

**Spoilage bacteria** make food smell, taste or look bad, but rarely make you ill. **Pathogenic bacteria**, such as *Listeria* and *Salmonella*, cause food poisoning. As the FDA explains, food can make you very sick even when it doesn't look, smell or taste spoiled.

That's the logic behind the two dates used in the UK, EU, Australia and New Zealand. **Use by** is a safety date, set for foods where pathogens could grow. **Best before** is a quality date, for foods where the main risk is going stale. The UK Food Standards Agency (FSA) says the "sniff test" is fine for best-before food but should never be used for use-by food.

## Usually fine after "best before"

These foods lose quality slowly. Past the date, check the pack, then look, smell and taste a little.

- **Dry goods:** pasta, rice, flour, oats, cereal, crackers, biscuits and chips/crisps. They may go stale, and flour and whole grains can go rancid, but they rarely become unsafe if kept dry.
- **Canned food in good condition.** The USDA says commercially canned food is safe indefinitely if the can is undamaged, although texture and flavor decline over the years.
- **Frozen food.** According to the FDA, food kept frozen at 0°F (-18°C) stays safe indefinitely. Freezer burn is a quality problem, not a safety one.
- **Unopened condiments, honey, chocolate, jams and similar products.** These are mostly quality issues.

Throw them out if you see mold, insects, damp, an odd smell or a damaged pack.

## Throw out after "use by"

For foods with a real safety-based use-by date, the date is the limit. The FSA advises against eating meat after its use-by date, even if it seems fine. Typical examples include:

- raw and cooked meat, poultry and fish, including smoked fish
- ready-to-eat salads, sandwiches and deli meats
- soft cheeses, milk and yogurt when they carry a use-by date
- **infant formula**, in every country

You don't have to eat use-by food on the day. You can **freeze it on or before the date**, or cook it before the date and eat it within 48 hours (FSA guidance).

**In the US**, where perishables often carry "sell by" or quality dates, use storage times instead. The FDA's storage chart gives, for example, **1–2 days** in the fridge for raw ground meat and raw poultry, **3–5 days** for opened lunch meat and **3–4 days** for cooked leftovers.

## Canned food: warning signs

Throw away a can, **without tasting the food**, if:

- it's **bulging, swollen or leaking**
- it has a **deep dent**, especially on the seam or rim, or heavy rust
- liquid or foam **spurts out** when you open it
- the food is discolored, moldy or smells bad

The CDC warns that these can be signs of *Clostridium botulinum*, the bacterium that causes botulism. You can't see, smell or taste the toxin, and even a small taste can be deadly. The FSA adds that a shallow dent is usually fine if the can shows no other damage. Improperly home-canned vegetables and meats carry the highest risk.

## Mold: when to cut and when to toss

The USDA's rule of thumb is that mold on **hard, dense food** usually can't spread far, but on **soft or moist food** its threads can reach well below the surface, along with bacteria you can't see.

- **Cut it off** (at least 1 inch, or about 2.5 cm, around and below the spot, keeping the knife out of the mold): hard cheese such as cheddar or Parmesan, and firm fruits and vegetables such as carrots, cabbage and bell peppers.
- **Throw it out:** soft cheese, yogurt and sour cream, bread and baked goods, jam and jelly, soft fruits and vegetables, cooked leftovers, pasta and grains, lunch meat, bacon and hot dogs, peanut butter, and nuts.

Don't sniff moldy food. Breathing in the spores can cause respiratory problems.

## The 2-hour rule

Bacteria multiply fastest between **40°F and 140°F (4°C and 60°C)**, the "danger zone". The FDA and USDA advise:

- Don't leave perishable food, such as meat, poultry, seafood, eggs, dairy, cut fruit and leftovers, at room temperature for **more than 2 hours**.
- If it's **above 90°F (32°C)**, for example at a picnic or in a hot car, the limit is **1 hour**.
- If food has been out longer, throw it away. Reheating won't reliably make it safe, because some bacteria produce toxins that heat doesn't destroy.

The UK's FSA similarly says to get cooked food into the fridge within one to two hours.

## Quick reference

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Pasta or crackers a few weeks past best before, pack intact | Check them; usually fine |
| Can past best before, can in good shape | Check when opening; if anything seems off, throw it out |
| Bulging, leaking or deeply dented can | Throw it out; don't open or taste |
| Chicken one day past use by (UK/EU/AU) | Throw it out |
| Milk past its date, smells fine | Use-by date: throw it out. Best-before date: check before using |
| Infant formula past its date | Throw it out |
| Bread with a few spots of mold | Throw out the whole loaf |
| Not sure what kind of date it is | Treat it as a use-by date |

## If you think you've eaten something bad

Most food poisoning passes on its own, but seek medical help for severe or lasting symptoms, such as high fever, bloody diarrhea or signs of dehydration. **Blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, or difficulty swallowing or breathing** after eating canned or preserved food can be signs of botulism. The CDC treats this as a medical emergency: go to the emergency room straight away.
