# How to organize your fridge and pantry to waste less food

Most food that gets thrown away at home wasn't bad food. It was forgotten: the yogurt pushed behind the milk, the half bag of spinach at the bottom of the drawer, the jar of pasta sauce bought twice.

The numbers add up. The UN Environment Programme's Food Waste Index Report 2024 estimates that households threw away 631 million tonnes of food in 2022, about 60% of all food wasted at the retail, food service and household level. In the US, the FDA estimates that 30–40% of the food supply goes uneaten. In the UK, WRAP puts the value of edible food a four-person household throws away at about £1,000 a year. By weight, 39% of it was binned because it wasn't used in time: it smelled or looked off (22%), or it was past the date on the label (17%).

The fix is less about willpower and more about a kitchen where you can see what needs eating.

> **The short version**
>
> - **First in, first out:** new food goes to the back, older food to the front.
> - **Label everything** that doesn't come with a date: leftovers, frozen food, decanted dry goods.
> - **Give each fridge zone a job**, and keep raw meat on the bottom shelf.
> - **Set up an "eat first" shelf** for anything close to its date.
> - **Plan a few meals and shop with a list**, after checking what you already have.
> - **Check the fridge temperature:** 40°F (4°C) or below in the US, 0–5°C in the UK.

## 1. First in, first out (FIFO)

Shops rotate stock so older products sell first. Do the same at home. When you unpack the shopping, move what's already there to the front and put the new items behind it. The USDA recommends this approach because food is less likely to spoil when older items get used first.

FIFO works best when you can actually see things:

- Use **clear containers** for leftovers and dry goods.
- Don't overfill the fridge. The FDA points out that cold air has to circulate for food to stay properly chilled.
- Keep **one kind of item in one place**, so you can tell at a glance you already have two jars of mustard.

## 2. Give each part of the fridge a job

Temperatures vary inside a fridge, and a layout you stick to makes it easier to notice what's there.

- **Upper shelves:** ready-to-eat food such as leftovers, deli items, yogurt and cooked dishes.
- **Bottom shelf:** raw meat, poultry and fish, in sealed containers or their packaging. The UK Food Standards Agency (FSA) recommends this so juices can't drip onto other food.
- **Crisper drawers:** vegetables and fruit.
- **Door:** condiments, juice and other items that cope with small temperature swings. The FDA advises keeping eggs in their carton on a shelf rather than in the door, because the door is warmer.

Check the temperature with an inexpensive fridge thermometer. The FDA recommends 40°F (4°C) or below for the fridge and 0°F (-18°C) for the freezer. The FSA says 0–5°C. WRAP's research suggests the average UK fridge runs at around 7°C, which is too warm, and turning it down helps food last longer.

## 3. Label what doesn't have a date

Leftovers, portions you freeze yourself and food decanted into jars lose their dates the moment they leave the pack. Keep a roll of masking tape and a marker in the kitchen and write two things: **what it is** and **the date**. The FSA suggests labeling frozen food so you don't end up with "unidentified frozen objects".

How long leftovers keep depends on whose advice you follow:

- USDA and FDA storage charts give most cooked leftovers **3–4 days** in the fridge.
- The FSA is stricter: eat leftovers **within 48 hours** or freeze them. For cooked rice, it advises **24 hours**.

If you're not sure which rule applies, follow the shorter one.

## 4. Make an "eat first" shelf

Pick one spot, such as a shelf at eye level or a small box, for food that needs using within the next few days. Opened packs, the last two eggs and vegetables that are starting to soften all go there. When anyone opens the fridge looking for a snack or planning dinner, that's where they look first.

## 5. Use the freezer on purpose

The freezer is the easiest way to buy time. According to the FDA, food kept at 0°F (-18°C) stays safe indefinitely, although its quality slowly drops. In the UK, the FSA says you can freeze food right up to its use-by date. Once food is defrosted, use it within 24 hours.

Freeze bread in slices, meat in meal-sized portions and leftovers in flat bags, so they thaw quickly and you only take out what you need.

## 6. Plan meals and shop with a list

The FDA's advice for cutting food waste starts before you reach the shop:

- Check the fridge and pantry first, so you don't buy what you already have.
- Plan meals and write a shopping list.
- Be wary of bulk buys and promotions, especially for produce and dairy with a short shelf life.

You don't need a full weekly menu. Planning three or four dinners around what's already in the fridge, and leaving a night for leftovers, covers most of it.

## 7. Give the pantry the same treatment

Dry goods last a long time, which is exactly why they get forgotten. Group similar items together, keep older packs in front and move opened flour, rice and cereal into sealed containers. The USDA recommends a cool, dry place for shelf-stable food, ideally below 85°F (29°C), and away from the stove and oven.

## Weekly 10-minute fridge check

- [ ] Take out anything past its safe window and throw it away.
- [ ] Wipe up spills, especially any drips from raw meat.
- [ ] Move food close to its date onto the eat-first shelf.
- [ ] Freeze anything you won't get to in time.
- [ ] Plan the next few meals around what's left.
- [ ] Add what's run out to the shopping list.
- [ ] When in doubt, throw it out.
